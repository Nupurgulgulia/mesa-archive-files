The Framework
1
Problem

Say their situation back to them, before you say anything about yourself.

Test: would they nod?

2
Solution

Connect what you do to the problem you just named.

Test: could this sentence be deleted from your website and nobody notice?

3
Proof

Evidence that this worked for someone like them.

Test: is the proof about you, or about them?

4
Ask

One specific next step, sized to where they actually are.

Test: can they say yes today, without asking anyone?

Why the Order Matters More Than the Content

Almost every bad pitch is a good pitch with the parts in the wrong sequence.

Lead with Solution

They have to guess which problem you think they have. They guess wrong.

Lead with Proof

It sounds like a credentials check.

Lead with Ask

That is a cold call, not a pitch.

The Problem has to come first because it is the only part the buyer can verify from their own experience.

Everything after it is a claim. The Problem is what earns you the right to make claims.

The Problem: The Part Everyone Gets Wrong
Write it in the second person, about something that actually happened.

Not: "Companies your size often struggle to manage this at scale."

That is a market statement. It describes a category, and they have to be persuaded they belong in it.

Instead: "When that happened in March, you found out about it from someone outside the business."

That is an event. They cannot disagree with it.

Three rules
Second person, not third
A specific moment, not a general condition
Your product does not appear anywhere in it
Proof: About You, or About Them?
Proof about you

Customer count, funding, investors, review scores, logos, years in business.

It answers "are you a real company?" — a question they stopped asking once they agreed to the meeting.

Proof about them

One buyer with the same problem, what changed, and how long it took.

It answers "has this worked for someone in my situation?" — which is the question they are actually holding.

The strongest proof carries a detail the buyer would recognise, and a line about how it felt rather than what it did.

The Ask: Size It to Where They Are

The most common failure is an Ask that is technically specific and practically impossible.

Too big

"Shall we set up a 30-minute call with your leadership team?"

Requires someone who is not in the room.

Too vague

"Let me know if this is interesting."

Costs them nothing to ignore.

Right size

One thing they can agree to alone, today, that is useful to them whether or not they buy.

The test: can they say yes without asking anyone?

One Pitch, One Person

The same product, sold into the same company, needs a different pitch for each person in the decision.

The person who signs

Cares about money leaving the business, and about not being wrong.

The person who runs it

Cares about what lands on their desk when it goes wrong.

The person who uses it

Cares about whether it works in their actual day.

Writing one pitch for all three produces a pitch for none of them.

The test: if your pitch survives a change of buyer without edits, it is a brochure.

Objections Are Not Attacks

An objection tells you which part of your pitch the buyer did not believe.

Where it lands

	

What it means

	

What to fix




Problem

	

They don't recognise the situation you described

	

You guessed instead of listening. Go back to discovery.




Solution

	

They believe the problem, not that you solve it

	

You claimed a capability without connecting it. Narrow it.




Proof

	

They believe you solve it, not for someone like them

	

Your proof is about you. Replace it with a peer.




Ask

	

They agree with everything and still won't commit

	

Too big, or it needs someone not in the room.

A pitch that draws no objection has usually been too vague to disagree with.

The Workshop
1
Round 1 — Draft (18 min)

Write your 60-second pitch. Four parts. Write it to be spoken. Read it aloud under your breath and time it before you stop editing.

2
Round 2 — Deliver (22 min)

Pairs, 11 minutes each way. Your partner plays the buyer and raises exactly one objection — the thing they least believed.

You do not answer it. Write it down verbatim. This is the hardest rule today and it is deliberate.

3
Round 3 — Rewrite (13 min)

Handle the objection inside the pitch, before it is raised. Do not add a fifth section beginning "You might be wondering…"
