Mom Test - Bad Questions

Type

	

Bad Questions

	

Why They’re Bad




Bad Question 1

	

Do you think this is a good idea?

	

It seeks validation and puts the customer in a position to be polite rather than honest.




Bad Question 2

	

Would you use a product that does [specific feature]?

	

It asks for speculative feedback and doesn’t reflect actual behavior.




Bad Question 3

	

How much would you pay for a tool that solves [specific problem]?

	

It’s too early to discuss pricing and can lead to inaccurate responses.




Bad Question 4

	

Do you think this feature would be useful?

	

It leads the customer to give a positive answer, whether or not they truly believe it.




Bad Question 5

	

Would you like a tool that makes [specific task] easier?

	

It’s a leading question that assumes the customer’s needs and preferences.




Bad Question 6

	

Do you think this is better than what you currently use?

	

It asks for a comparison and can make the customer defensive about their current choices.




Bad Question 7

	

Is this something you’d be interested in?

	

It’s too vague and speculative, not grounded in their actual behavior or needs.




Bad Question 8

	

How would you feel if you couldn’t use this product?

	

It’s hypothetical and doesn’t provide actionable insights.




Bad Question 9

	

Would you recommend this to your friends?

	

It seeks validation and social proof without understanding the underlying problems.




Bad Question 10

	

Do you like this feature?

	

It focuses on personal opinion rather than practical utility and real-world use.

Introduction to SPIN Selling

SPIN Selling aligns with "The Mom Test" - it uses questions to understand the buyer, identify their pain, and demonstrate value.

 The four key types of SPIN questions are:

Situation Questions: Understand the buyer's current circumstances and context.
Problem Questions: Identify the buyer's specific pain points and challenges.
Implication Questions: Explore the consequences and impact of the buyer's problems.
Need-Payoff Questions: Demonstrate the value and benefits of solving the buyer's problems.

Situation Questions Examples

These questions help you understand the customer's current situation, processes, and needs. By asking them, you gain insights to tailor your solution and sales approach.

Sample Situation Questions:

Identifying Customer Problems

These questions identify and explore problems the customer is facing.

Sample Identification Questions:

What challenges are you encountering with your current system?
Are there any inefficiencies in your current process that concern you?
What are the biggest obstacles preventing you from achieving your goals?
How satisfied are you with your current [system/process]?
Have you experienced any issues with [specific aspect] lately?
What are the main pain points your team faces with [specific task]?
Is there anything about your current solution that you find frustrating?
Implication Questions

These questions help the customer understand the consequences of their problems.

Sample Implication Questions:

What impact do these challenges have on your overall productivity?
How do these issues affect your bottom line?
What could happen if these problems are not addressed soon?
How do these inefficiencies affect your team’s morale?
What are the potential risks if these issues persist?
How much time/money do you think you’re losing due to this problem?
How do these challenges impact your ability to meet customer expectations?
Need-Payoff Questions 

These questions highlight the benefits of solving the problems.

Sample Need-Payoff Questions:

What would achieving your goals mean for your company?

Introduction to Challenger Sales

The Challenger Sales approach, developed by Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson, emphasizes three key elements: teaching, tailoring, and taking control. 

The teaching aspect involves providing customers with insights that reframe their thinking, challenging their status quo. 
1.
Tailoring the message to the customer's specific needs is also crucial. 
2.
Control - encourages sales professionals to drive the conversation towards a decision, taking an active role in guiding the customer.
3.

This proactive, insight-driven approach to sales contrasts with more passive, reactive selling methods. 

By challenging customers and offering valuable perspectives, the Challenger Sales technique aligns well with the need for asking insightful questions to uncover customer needs and pain points.

Challenger Sales Approach

Scenario: Introducing a new cybersecurity solution to an IT director

1. Teaching

The Challenger Sales approach involves educating the customer on industry trends and insights. For example, "Did you know that 60% of small businesses close within six months of a cyber attack? Our research shows that most current solutions only address 40% of potential vulnerabilities."

2. Tailoring

The Challenger Sales rep then tailors the message to the customer's specific needs and pain points. "Given your company's rapid growth and increasing data volume, a tailored cybersecurity solution is crucial to protect your assets."

3. Taking Control

Finally, the Challenger Sales rep takes control of the conversation and steers it towards a decision. "To ensure your company isn't exposed to these risks, let's discuss how our advanced solution can provide comprehensive protection and peace of mind."

Common Mistakes to Avoid
Pitching your idea instead of listening

Mistake: Trying to sell your idea during the interview instead of focusing on understanding the customer's problems and needs. 

Solution: Keep the focus on the customer and ask open-ended questions to learn more about their challenges.

Asking leading questions

Mistake: Framing questions in a way that suggests a desired answer. 

Solution: Ask open-ended questions that allow for genuine, unbiased responses from the customer.

Seeking validation instead of truth

Mistake: Looking for positive feedback to validate your idea rather than seeking honest, critical feedback that can help you improve. 

Solution: Be open to constructive criticism and use it to refine your approach.

Failing to dig deeper into responses

Mistake: Not probing further when a customer gives a vague or brief answer. 

Solution: Ask follow-up questions to get to the root of the issue and uncover the customer's true needs.

Practical Example: Integrating SPIN and Challenger Selling 

In this scenario, you are selling a new customer relationship management (CRM) software to a mid-sized company struggling with customer retention. The office has a professional, focused atmosphere with employees diligently working at their desks.

Your goal is to understand the company's specific challenges around customer service and demonstrate how your CRM solution can improve their processes and increase customer loyalty.

SPIN and Challenger selling
Situation Analysis (SPIN)
 Question: "Can you describe your current process for managing customer relationships?"
Customer: "We use an outdated CRM system that doesn't integrate well with our other tools."
Problem Identification (SPIN)
Question: "What challenges do you face with your current CRM system?"
Customer: "It's slow, lacks important features, and our team finds it frustrating to use.
Implication Exploration (SPIN)
Question: "How do these challenges affect your customer retention and overall business performance?"
Customer: "Due to the inefficiencies, our customer service team spends too much time on administrative tasks instead of engaging with customers, leading to lower customer satisfaction and higher churn rates."
Need-Payoff (SPIN)
Question: "If you could solve these challenges with a more efficient CRM, what impact would that have on your team and customer retention?"
Customer: "It would free up time for our team to focus on building relationships with customers, likely improving satisfaction and reducing churn. It would also streamline our processes, making us more efficient overall."
SPIN Selling and Challenger selling
Teaching Moment (Challenger)
Objective: Provide new insights that challenge the customer’s current thinking and add value to the conversation.
Action: Share relevant industry data and insights.
Example: "Did you know that companies that switch to a modern CRM system see, on average, a 20% increase in customer satisfaction within the first year? Our studies have shown that integrated systems significantly reduce administrative workload and improve customer engagement."
Tailoring the Message (Challenger)
Objective: Customize your solution to the specific needs and situation of the customer.
Action: Relate the benefits of your CRM directly to the customer’s pain points and desired outcomes.
Example: "Given your team’s challenges with inefficiency and customer churn, our CRM offers seamless integration with your current tools, robust automation features to reduce administrative tasks, and advanced analytics to help you understand and engage with your customers better. This directly addresses the issues you’ve been facing and aligns with your goals of improving customer retention and satisfaction."
Taking Control (Challenger)
Objective: Steer the conversation towards a commitment and the next steps.
Action: Summarize the discussion and propose a clear next step to move forward.
Example: "Based on everything we've discussed, it's evident that our CRM can resolve your current inefficiencies and help you achieve your customer retention goals. To see how our solution can specifically benefit your team, let's schedule a demo next week. How does Tuesday at 10 AM work for you?"
