Setup

The case

You are not selling today. You are reviewing calls that someone else made, the way a sales manager does every Monday morning.

The seller
GoKwik — e-commerce enablement for Indian D2C brands. KwikCheckout, a one-click checkout that reduces cart abandonment. RTO Shield, which scores every cash-on-delivery order for the risk the customer refuses the parcel, so high-risk orders can be verified, converted to prepaid, or declined before they ship.
The problem
RTO — return to origin. A refused COD parcel goes back to the warehouse. The brand pays shipping both ways and gets nothing. It rarely shows as a line item, so most brands under-count it.
Market numbers
COD is 40–60% of orders. RTO runs 15–30% on COD. Roughly ₹120–250 cost per RTO plus blocked inventory. Fits brands doing more than about 1,500 orders a month.
Your job
Build an agent that reads a raw transcript and returns a structured deal review: what was established, what was missed, how healthy the deal is, and what the rep should do next.

This is the most delegated task in modern sales operations, and for a simple reason: it is high volume, pattern-based, and nobody has time to listen to forty calls a week. It is also the task where an agent has one genuine advantage over the person who made the call.

The advantage

The agent was not in the room. It does not like the buyer, it did not enjoy the conversation, and it has nothing invested in the deal being real. Hold on to that — it is the whole reason this works, and it comes back at the end.

Faculty note

Do not hand out transcripts B and C until minute 30. If pairs see all three during the build, they will write a prompt that recognises these specific calls rather than one that generalises — and the whole Part 3 comparison collapses. Transcript A is the only one on the table during Part 2.

Setup

The scoring model

Everyone uses this exact model. Different prompts, same scale — otherwise the comparison at minute 40 means nothing.

The five things a manager checks

CriterionA healthy answerThe failure
NeedA number, a frequency, or an incident. “RTO is running 24% and we lost about eleven lakh last quarter.”“They're interested in reducing RTO.” That is a topic, not a need.
Economic buyerA name, a role, and how they decide.“The contact will take it internally.” To whom? Nobody knows.
TimelineA dated external event — a season, an audit, a contract end.“They said Q4.” A quarter is not an event.
Next stepOne specific thing, with an owner and a date, agreed out loud by both sides.“I'll send the deck.” One-sided, undated, requires nothing of the buyer.
Multi-threadingTwo or more people, across functions.One contact. Single-threaded deals die when that person gets busy or overruled.
The signal that matters more than any other

Whether the buyer did something, or only said something. Enthusiasm is not a signal. Agreeing to pull data is a signal. Booking a date is a signal. Introducing a colleague is a signal.

Every deal that dies at 90% was forecast on things a buyer said.

The scale

ScoreMeansTest
0Not establishedThe rep never asked, or asked and got nothing usable.
1PartialTouched on, but no number, no name, no date.
2EstablishedA specific, quotable fact exists in the transcript.
Total /10BandWhat it means on Monday morning
8–10HealthyForecastable. Work it.
4–7At riskReal but incomplete. Name the gap and close it before the next stage.
0–3UnqualifiedNot a deal yet. Requalify it or take it out of the pipeline.
The rule that makes the agent usable

Every score must be justified with a verbatim quote from the transcript, or with the words NO EVIDENCE IN TRANSCRIPT. No paraphrasing. No inference. No “the buyer seemed”.

If the agent cannot quote it, the score is 0. This one constraint is the difference between a review a manager would act on and a plausible-sounding summary. Build it into the first line of your prompt, and repeat it.

Workshop 3

Deal health · 55 minutes

00–10

What a manager is actually checking

Laptops closed for the first five minutes. You are reading, not building.

  1. Read transcript A

    It is at the bottom of this page. Four minutes. Read it properly — you are about to score it by hand and then again with an agent.

  2. Score it out loud, as a pair, against the five criteria

    0, 1 or 2 on each. Say the quote you are scoring on. If you cannot find a quote, it is a 0.

  3. Write your total somewhere you will not lose it

    You will compare it to your agent's score at minute 32.

Before you move on

Transcript A should score high. If you have it below 8, go back — you are being harsh on something, and finding out what now will save you twenty minutes later.

Faculty note

Take two pairs' scores for A at minute 8 and put them on the board. They should agree within a point. If they do not, the disagreement is almost always on Next Step — some pairs score the booked meeting as a 2, others want to see it confirmed. Settle it now: it was agreed out loud with a date and the buyer took an action, so it is a 2. Calibrating on A is what makes C meaningful.

05–10

Practice — spot what the agent made up

Before you build one, look at what a badly built one produces.

Below is output from an agent reviewing a call. Three of these six lines are not supported by anything in the transcript it read. Click the ones you would refuse to put in front of a sales manager.

The three ways this agent failed

It invented a number. It scored warmth as evidence. It attached an invented conclusion to a real fact. All three read fluently. All three would survive a skim. Your prompt has to make each one impossible.

Faculty note

The sentiment line is the one to dwell on. Most pairs flag the budget invention immediately and miss the sentiment line entirely, because it reads like reasonable sales language. Ask the room: what would this agent do with a call where the buyer was delightful and committed to nothing? That question sets up transcript C thirty minutes before they see it.

10–30

Build the agent

In pairs. You get the contract — the output schema. You write the instructions. That is the exercise.

  1. Open a new chat

    Any assistant. No web search needed — everything the agent needs is in the transcript you paste.

  2. Copy the schema block below into your prompt, verbatim

    This is the contract, not the prompt. Every pair uses the same schema so the scores are comparable.

  3. Write the rules yourselves

    The skeleton block has four sections marked [YOU WRITE THIS]. Those four are where the agent is actually built. Copying someone else's wording here defeats the point.

  4. Test on transcript A. Then test on transcript A again.

    Two runs, same transcript, same prompt. If the two scores disagree, your instructions are ambiguous — fix them now, not at minute 35.

  5. Check it against your hand score from Part 1

    Within one point is fine. Three points apart means one of you is wrong and it is worth finding out which.

How agents fail at this task

The failureWhat it looks likeWhat to build in
HallucinationStates a budget, headcount or timeline that is nowhere in the transcript. The most dangerous failure, because the output reads well.The verbatim-quote rule, stated more than once, plus an explicit ban on inference.
GenerosityEverything scores 7 or 8. The model is agreeable by default and reluctant to call a deal bad.Tell it 0 is a common and correct score, and that NO EVIDENCE IN TRANSCRIPT is the expected answer on a weak call.
Rapport-scoringA friendly, fluent call scores highly because it sounds successful.State that tone, warmth and buyer enthusiasm are not evidence and must never raise a score.
Vague coaching“The rep should ask more discovery questions.” True of every call ever recorded.Require a quoted line from the rep and a specific replacement.
Not-asked vs asked-and-failedBoth score 0 with no distinction, so the coaching is wrong.Make the agent label which it was. They need different feedback.
Before minute 30

Two runs on transcript A agreeing within a point, and nothing in your prompt that mentions footwear, Ritika, or festive season. If your prompt knows anything about this specific call, it will fail on the next two.

Faculty note

Circulate and read the WHAT IS NOT EVIDENCE section on four or five prompts. The pairs who write something real there — naming tone, enthusiasm, agreement, politeness — will score transcript C correctly in fifteen minutes. The pairs who leave it thin will not. You can predict the Part 3 results from this one section, and it is worth telling them so at minute 25 without saying why.

40–45

Calibrate

Enter what you and your agent gave transcript C.

—Enter both scores.
Ground truth

Transcript C scores 3 out of 10. Need 2, everything else 0 except Next Step, which is a 1 because something was agreed — one-sided, undated, and requiring nothing of the buyer.

It is the second-longest call of the three. The buyer is enthusiastic throughout. The rep is fluent. It ends warmly. And almost nothing was established.

Faculty note

Do not reveal the ground truth until every pair has entered their numbers. The calibrator is designed so that a high hand score and a low agent score produces the most useful message — that is the majority case and it is the moment the workshop lands.

45–55

Debrief

Four questions. The last one is not about agents.

  1. What did the agent catch that you missed on your own first read?
  2. What did it get wrong, or state that was not in the transcript?

    Including on transcript A. There is a real risk in A that a lot of agents miss — the economic buyer is named and has never been spoken to.

  3. Where does a human still need to override this agent?

    Name a specific decision you would not delegate to it.

  4. What have you taken from this course that applies outside a sales role?

    One thing, and where you would use it. This is the last AI workshop of the course.

A question worth sitting with

Your agent scored transcript C in a few seconds, and probably scored it correctly. The rep who made that call thought it went well and would have forecast it. So would most people in this room on a first read.

The agent was not smarter than you. It was just unable to be charmed.

Faculty note · running the debrief

1. Put every pair's transcript C score on the board. Expect 2 to 8.

2. Ask the pair with the highest score to read out what their agent cited as evidence for Need or Next Step. It will almost always be an enthusiasm quote. Let the room hear it.

3. Ask: how many of you scored C higher by hand than your agent did? Usually a majority. This is the moment.

4. Ask what the agent got wrong on A — Ritika named but unmet. Calibration cuts both ways and this prevents the conclusion that the agent is always right.

5. The override question. Good answers: whether to disqualify, anything needing account knowledge from outside the call, judging whether a buyer is lying.

6. Close on the course question. Give it the full two minutes.

The line to end on

The agent did not outperform you because it understands sales better than you do. It outperformed you because it was not in the room, did not like the buyer, and had no reason to want the deal to be real.

Every rep who has forecast a deal that died knew, somewhere, what transcript C was. The agent's only advantage is that it has nothing invested in the answer — and that is worth knowing about every judgement you will make in a commercial role.

FARHAN: Shopify for the store, Shiprocket for fulfilment, and we put in a returns platform last March.

ADITYA: Does the returns platform touch any of this?

FARHAN: Not yet. But she pulled the July numbers, so she knows the problem better than I do. She'll be more sceptical than me, fair warning.

ADITYA: That's useful to know. How did you end up buying the returns platform last March?

FARHAN: Six lakh a year. I found it, Ritika pushed back for about three weeks, then approved it off the ops budget once we showed her what it was saving on manual processing.

Faculty note · ground truth for all three

A = 10. Need 2 (24% on COD, ₹11 lakh last quarter, 31% in the peak fortnight, plus a failed internal fix). Economic buyer 2 (Ritika — named, function stated, and how she decides: “a spreadsheet… before and after on our actual numbers”). Timeline 2 (festive in ten weeks, with last year's evidence). Next step 2 (template today, export by Friday, dated meeting on the 14th, buyer forwarding the invite). Multi-threading 2 (Ritika identified and being brought into the meeting).

Risk that still exists in A: Ritika has never been spoken to, she is the sceptic, and the rep has committed to answering a downtime question he has not yet answered. An agent that scores A a 10 with no risk flags is miscalibrated in the other direction.

B = 0. Nothing established. The only question asked all call is a leading one — “You must be seeing pretty high RTO, right?” — which returns “I guess so, yeah.” That is the cleanest example in the pack of asked-and-failed rather than not-asked, and it is the coaching point: a closed leading question produces a worthless answer and the rep never follows up. “Send something across” is not a next step.

C = 3. Need 2 (20% RTO, 62% COD, 11,000 orders — specific enough, even though she cannot cost it). Economic buyer 0. Timeline 0. Next step 1. Multi-threading 0.

The three near-misses in C — this is what the expanded transcript is for. Each one looks like a score and is not:

1. Dinesh is named. “Dinesh tracks it — he's our ops lead.” A second person exists, but the rep never asks to speak to him and never mentions him again. Multi-threading counts people the rep has actually engaged, so this stays 0. Agents that score it 1 or 2 are counting mentions rather than relationships.

2. November is a date. “Our planning cycle runs in November so things are a bit tight until then.” It is a real date and it is not a forcing event for this decision — it is context about her own budget, and if anything it is a reason to delay. Timeline stays 0. This is the sharpest discrimination test in the pack.

3. The rep offers to multi-thread and is deflected. “I'm happy to jump on a call with anyone else who needs convincing” → “Maybe, yeah. Let me see where it lands first.” The offer was made and declined, which is information — but it establishes nothing, and the next step is still one-sided and undated.

Note also that Vikas does more visible discovery than in the shorter version — he asks volume, COD share, RTO rate and tech stack. The call looks better than the original and still scores 3. That is deliberate: a well-built agent should be unmoved by the extra activity, because none of it touched authority, urgency or commitment.

The highest-value unasked question in C: “You mentioned you looked at something similar last year and it didn't go anywhere — what happened?” It would have surfaced the decision process, the real blocker, and probably the economic buyer, in one answer. Agents that name a generic qualification question here are correct but less insightful.
